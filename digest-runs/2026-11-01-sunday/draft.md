## Reflection: Burdens Are Carried by People

Paul wrote this letter to young churches he had planted, and he wrote it with urgency. Some teachers had told these believers they had to keep the law of Moses to truly belong to God's people. For most of the letter, Paul answers that we are made right with God through faith in Christ, not by keeping rules.

Then, near the end, he does something surprising. He brings the word "law" back. Right after urging the church to restore anyone caught in sin with a gentle spirit, he writes: "Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."

The law of Christ is not a new checklist. It is the way of Jesus, who carried our heaviest burden to the cross. Paul says we keep that law most clearly when we pick up the weight someone else is carrying. A burden here is anything too heavy to hold alone. Guilt, grief, doubt and fear all qualify.

Notice that Paul gives this command to a church, not to one gifted helper. Burden-bearing happens between people who know each other's names. It takes time and presence, and sometimes it is inconvenient. It cannot be handed off entirely to someone else, or to something else.

That matters this week, as Christian ministries build tools that answer our questions at any hour. A good tool can point us toward help. The help Paul describes still has a face.

So here is one practice for this week. Think of one person in your church or family who seems to be carrying something heavy. Reach out by name. Ask how you can help, and then stay long enough to listen.

## Feature: Ask Ligonier, a Ministry AI Built to Hand You to a Real Person

Ligonier Ministries, a Bible-teaching ministry, is testing an AI assistant that answers faith questions from its own teaching library. Its chief executive says it will "defer to a real human" when users push past its limits [[1]](#source-1). The tool, Ask Ligonier, is in a limited beta, mainly for Ligonier's Ministry Partners, with a 30-day trial offered through the Grow app [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4).

Peter D'Abrosca of Fox News Digital reported on the tool on Oct 31, with on-record comments from Ligonier president and CEO Chris Larson [[1]](#source-1). Fox calls the app "Grow" and describes an "intelligent answer assistant" inside it [[1]](#source-1). Ligonier's own experiments site, Ligonier Labs, names that assistant Ask Ligonier. It says the assistant responds "to questions about the Bible, theology, and the Christian life" [[2]](#source-2).

It is not a public launch. Ligonier Labs says access to its experiments "is limited to select users during their testing phase" [[2]](#source-2). The Ask Ligonier page calls the tool "in-development" and says access "is currently limited to Ministry Partners during this beta phase" [[3]](#source-3). Grow's App Store notes for version 12.0.1 (Sept 17) say "This feature is available to Ministry Partners" [[5]](#source-5). The Grow website offers "30 free days of Ask Ligonier" [[4]](#source-4).

**How Ligonier says it works.** "It is just trained on our library," Larson told Fox [[1]](#source-1). He described "50 years of teaching" from "more than 800 different teachers," which he said is "grounded in Dr. R.C. Sproul's lifetime of teaching." Fox puts the library at "tens of thousands of pieces of content" [[1]](#source-1). Ligonier's own page describes the assistant as "drawing answers from Ligonier’s deep teaching library" [[3]](#source-3). Ligonier has not published which AI model runs underneath, or whether the system was trained on the library or simply looks answers up in it.

Larson described firm limits. He said Ligonier has "given it a sandbox to work within." He added that "any time that somebody tries to push it beyond the borders, we're going to deflect or defer to a real human" [[1]](#source-1). A sandbox, in software, is a fenced-off space where a program can act only within set rules. If a user discusses suicidality, Fox reports, the assistant directs them to "the suicide hotline" [[1]](#source-1).

Larson also said the assistant is "not trying to pretend it's a person." He added: "We don't want it to take the place of a pastor or a local church elder or even a parent" [[1]](#source-1). Anyone building AI tools, he said, "needs to think through ethical guidelines" [[1]](#source-1).

The name is not new. In 2018, Ligonier launched a chat service called Ask Ligonier that was "staffed by a well-trained team" of people [[6]](#source-6). Ligonier has not said whether that team still operates or receives today's handoffs.

**Why the handoff matters.** Barna Group asked Christians and pastors when an AI should pass a conversation to a person, in research released Oct 22 with partner Gloo [[7]](#source-7). For mentions of suicide or self-harm, 94% of pastors and 63% of practicing Christians said AI should connect the person to a human right away [[7]](#source-7). The widest gap was on spiritual crisis. There, 71% of pastors wanted an immediate handoff, compared with 32% of practicing Christians, and 23% of practicing Christians said AI can continue [[7]](#source-7). Barna surveyed 1,514 adults, 683 of them practicing Christians, in November 2025, and 442 Protestant pastors in December 2025 [[7]](#source-7).

A theology assistant is a likely place for conversations about doubt to land. Barna also reported on Oct 6 that 79% of pastors say their church has neither a formal AI policy nor informal guidance [[8]](#source-8). Only 10% feel positive about AI providing spiritual counseling [[8]](#source-8).

**What is still unknown.** Every guardrail described here comes from Larson, through Fox. We found no outside test or review of Ask Ligonier. Barna's own caution applies: "The human handoff only works if there's someone to hand off to," said Barna's Daniel Copeland [[7]](#source-7). Barna notes that AI tools have usually offered "a referral, not a connection" [[7]](#source-7). Ligonier has not said who the "real human" is, or whether a handoff connects someone live.

Ligonier invites users to "share your feedback directly within the experiment" [[3]](#source-3). We found no date for wider access.

## Also Encouraging: Devout Teens and AI, and a Screen-Free Faith Ring

**Devout teens find AI a safe place to ask.** Barna surveyed 1,500 teens ages 13 to 18 in February, with Christ In Youth [[9]](#source-9). Among its "Faith-Activated" teens, 46% strongly agree AI feels like a safe place for questions they wouldn't ask out loud, versus 28% of all teens [[9]](#source-9). Parents still come first: teens said they would feel most comfortable asking mom (84%) and dad (78%) for advice, with AI at 35% [[9]](#source-9). Youth pastor Richard Hamilton says, "Our job is to make sure a trusted adult, not an algorithm, is in that space" [[9]](#source-9).

**A faith ring with no screen.** The team behind the Bible app Blessed, which it says has more than 10 million users, built the Elah Ring S1 [[10]](#source-10). The company says its Cross-Light and a gentle vibration provide a screen-free interaction [[10]](#source-10). It describes its app companion, FaithMate, as "a companion for a life of faith, not a replacement for a pastor, prayer, Scripture, church, or meaningful relationships" [[10]](#source-10). This is a company release distributed through HackerNoon's Business Blogging Program, and we found no independent coverage [[10]](#source-10).

## Wisdom Corner: Confident Ministry AI Answers Can Still Be Wrong

A ministry-trained assistant can sound authoritative and still be mistaken. It answers instantly, in a calm voice, at any hour. That is a real gift to a curious reader, and a real risk to someone in crisis who might confide in it instead of a pastor.

In January, researchers studying other Bible chatbots, not Ask Ligonier, found they "regularly framed one interpretive approach as definitive" [[11]](#source-11). Dr Jonas Kurlberg warned that AI answers "are often assumed to be neutral or factual," so people question them less [[11]](#source-11). Dr Zoltán Schwáb, quoted in the same report, asked whether "wrestling with the biblical text is often more important than receiving the answer to your question" [[11]](#source-11).

Proverbs says that "in the multitude of counselors there is victory" (Proverbs 11:14, World English Bible). An answer tool can be one voice among many. Let it send you back to your Bible, and to people who know you.
