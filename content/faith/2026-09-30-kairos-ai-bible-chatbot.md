## Reflection: A Reader on the Road Needs a Guide

In Acts 8:26-39, Luke tells the early church about a court official riding home from worship. He is reading the prophet Isaiah aloud. An angel sends Philip to the desert road, and the Spirit tells him to go near the chariot. Philip asks a plain question (Acts 8:30): “Do you understand what you are reading?”

The answer is honest (Acts 8:31). “How can I, unless someone explains it to me?” The official has Scripture in his hands and hunger in his heart. What he lacks is a person who can sit beside him and open the text. So he invites Philip up into the chariot.

Luke does not treat the text as the problem. The Word was already at work. But God's pattern here is personal. He sends a messenger, the messenger runs, and the two men read together. Before the chapter ends, Philip has told him the good news about Jesus, and the official asks to be baptized.

This week's feature is about a tool built for people who are reading alone, often with questions they are not ready to ask anyone. A chatbot can answer questions at any hour, and for a hesitant seeker that can be a real mercy. Acts 8 gives us a way to judge any such tool. Does it leave the seeker on the road alone with the text, or does it help bring a Philip alongside?

For most of us, the lesson this week has little to do with technology. Someone near you is reading and not understanding. Be willing to run to the chariot, and be ready to sit down when you are invited.

## Feature: Kairos, a Bible-Based AI Chatbot, Keeps Pointing Seekers to People

Kairos, a chatbot from the ministry Transform Iran, is an AI tool that answers questions about Christianity [[1]](#source-1). Transform Iran's Lana Silk told Mission Network News (MNN) how people are using it, in comments MNN reported Sep 24 [[1]](#source-1).

Kairos is not new; MNN described it as "newly launched" in August 2025 [[2]](#source-2). The news this week is Silk's account of what it is doing for the people who use it.

**How it works, according to Transform Iran.** Kairos answers from a closed library rather than the open web [[2]](#source-2)[[3]](#source-3). The ministry says "every response is carefully curated from Scripture and strictly vetted resources" [[4]](#source-4). Accounts of that library's size differ. A December 2025 Transform Iran newsroom post about a KMUZ Radio interview described its knowledge base as "over 5,000 articles, apologetics, and Scripture" [[3]](#source-3). A Transform Iran statement to The Christian Post in August cited "nearly 7,000 research articles" from its Porpasokh Apologetics Center [[5]](#source-5).

When Kairos is unsure, it is designed to say, "I do not know the answer to your question" [[3]](#source-3). Transform Iran says questions beyond its database are "flagged for human review and answered within 48 hours" [[4]](#source-4). The ministry also guards against the tool being mistaken for more than it is. It changed Kairos' wording after the tool began offering to pray for people, so users understood it "guides them in prayer, rather than replacing the role of God or a human pastor" [[3]](#source-3).

Transform Iran provides a free VPN, a service that hides a user's internet traffic, "to keep their spiritual inquiries safe" [[1]](#source-1).

One point goes unexplained. Transform Iran says "every response is monitored by Christian theologians and apologists" [[4]](#source-4). MNN describes the service as strictly anonymous unless a user chooses to say who they are [[1]](#source-1). The two could fit together if conversations are not tied to identities. None of the Transform Iran or MNN pages cited here explains how Kairos handles this [[1]](#source-1)[[3]](#source-3)[[4]](#source-4).

**Why some people choose it.** Silk told MNN that some seekers "didn't want the sort of pressure or judgment they were expecting to come from Christians in their own personal pursuit of truth." "Those kinds of people are choosing Kairos," she said [[1]](#source-1).

**What Transform Iran reports.** Silk described a woman whose marriage had broken down. Over about a week, Silk said, Kairos talked with her about guilt, shame and forgiveness and encouraged her to seek reconciliation. Then "it pushed her to then go and involve their pastor," and later to seek therapy [[1]](#source-1). "It just keeps pushing for the next step," Silk said [[1]](#source-1).

Transform Iran reports that a man it calls "Keyvan," a pseudonym, contacted Kairos in January, prayed as guided, and "reached out to a church community recommended by Kairos" [[6]](#source-6). Silk also told The Christian Post about a man who said, "I've chosen to follow Jesus" [[5]](#source-5). These accounts come from the ministry and cannot be independently checked.

**What's still open.** Every account of impact here comes from Transform Iran. Transform Iran says Silk has welcomed scrutiny from YouVersion founder Bobby Gruenewald [[3]](#source-3). The ministry is also plain about the tool's limits: "It is not a replacement for faith, discernment, or pastoral care" [[4]](#source-4).

## Also Encouraging: Scripture Set to Music

**Music Bible launches its app.** A team led by founder Micah Fiechter has used AI to set the entire Bible to music in 11 genres, plus three in Spanish [[7]](#source-7). The Music Bible app (themusicbible.org) launched Sep 22 on iOS and Android [[8]](#source-8). The company says it uses AI tools including Suno and ChatGPT for the music, never to interpret Scripture, though its own page notes that "occasionally, a word or phrase may be repeated for musical expression" [[9]](#source-9). Human reviewers check the songs, and Fiechter says flagged ones are pulled "within 24 hours" [[10]](#source-10). "I personally, at this moment in time, do not support the use of AI for interpreting Scripture," he said [[10]](#source-10).

## Wisdom Corner: An AI Chatbot Cannot Replace the Church

In an NPR/Ipsos poll of 1,011 adults, published by NPR on Sep 25 and updated Sep 28, 11% of AI users surveyed said they have sought spiritual guidance from AI, 13% have used it to generate prayers and 15% to study Scripture [[11]](#source-11). Nearly 7 in 10 AI users surveyed said they would never seek spiritual guidance from AI, and just 6% of all adults surveyed currently do [[11]](#source-11).

Accuracy is one worry. YouVersion's Bobby Gruenewald says even the best AI models misquote Scripture at least 15% of the time, and YouVersion has chosen not to launch a public-facing chatbot that answers theological questions [[12]](#source-12). He was describing open-ended AI chat models, not Kairos, but the standard is a fair one to apply: "we need it to be better in order to rely on it" [[12]](#source-12).

The deeper worry is belonging. Hebrews calls believers to keep encouraging one another, “not forsaking our own assembling together” (Hebrews 10:25). A tool can point the way to a church. It cannot be one.

## Prayer: Send Someone to the Road

Lord, as you sent Philip to the road, send faithful people to all who are reading your Word alone and afraid to ask. Let every tool we build serve that sending, and make us ready to sit beside the seeker. Amen.
