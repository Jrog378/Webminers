## Reflection: A Ready Heart and an Open Bible

Luke wrote the book of Acts for a man named Theophilus, to show how the good news about Jesus spread after the resurrection. In chapter 17, Paul reasons from the Scriptures in a synagogue, showing that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead. One group of listeners stands out.

Luke says they "received the word with all readiness of mind." Then they kept reading, "examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so."

Notice what Luke praises. These listeners were eager, not suspicious. Yet they did not take even an apostle's word on trust alone. They opened the Scriptures and checked. The next verse tells us that many of them believed.

That pairing of a ready heart and an open Bible is a good habit for this week. Answers to faith questions now come from many places: a sermon, a podcast, a friend and, more and more often, a chatbot. Some of those answers will be true and helpful. Some will sound confident and still be wrong.

The test is the one these listeners used. Take what you hear back to the text. Read it in context. Read it daily, and not only when you have a question.

Try one small practice in the days ahead. When something you hear about the Bible catches your attention, look up the passage yourself before you share it. Read the verses around it. Then talk about what you found with someone in your church, because Scripture was given to a people, to be read and weighed together.

## Feature: 70 Million Faith Questions a Week, and Where the Answers Come From

People bring questions about religion to ChatGPT more than 70 million times a week, OpenAI said on Oct 8, and about 22 million of those involve studying religious texts [[1]](#source-1). This month, Mission Network News (Oct 21) introduced unfoldingWord's free assistant BT Servant, which answers from a curated library of Bible resources, to general readers [[2]](#source-2).

**What OpenAI found.** The figures come from a new OpenAI report, *Faith, Community & AI*, summarized in the company's Global Affairs newsletter [[1]](#source-1). OpenAI says the 22 million text-study requests include "understanding difficult passages and exploring scholarly interpretations" [[1]](#source-1). Another 16 million a week concern practices, holidays and observance, and 16 million more focus on beliefs, meaning and spiritual guidance [[1]](#source-1). Christianity-related requests rise around Easter, the company says [[1]](#source-1).

The Christian Post, which says it reviewed OpenAI's analysis, wrote on Oct 20 that the data cover September 2025 to September 2026 [[3]](#source-3). By its account, about 34% of religion-related requests were about Christianity. Nearly half of those Christianity-related messages were about Bible passages, and another 13% were about worship, sermons or church practices [[3]](#source-3). Relevant reported the same shares on Oct 22 [[4]](#source-4).

"People ask ChatGPT to explain difficult passages, find relevant commentaries, and help them understand how their traditions approach particular questions," OpenAI researchers wrote, as quoted by the Christian Post [[3]](#source-3).

Some caution is in order. These are OpenAI's numbers about its own product. We could not find the full report, so the Bible-passage figures reach us through the Christian Post and Relevant. Neither the newsletter nor the coverage explains how requests were sorted. Nothing in them says these chats lead anyone to open a Bible or join a church.

**Where the answers come from.** If Scripture questions are already a main reason people bring faith to a chatbot, the source of the answers matters. That is the problem BT Servant was built to address.

Mission Network News reported in January that unfoldingWord had introduced the tool as an aid for church-centric Bible translators, drawing on what it called a "very specific, tightly controlled library of resources" [[5]](#source-5). On Oct 21, Mission Network News presented it to ordinary readers: "Now, the same tools that help Bible translators are available to you!" [[2]](#source-2)

BT Servant is free and works inside chat apps such as Signal and WhatsApp [[2]](#source-2). Its site says it runs "in the apps you already know," in several languages, with audio supported [[6]](#source-6). Developer Ian Lindsley says it is "as easy to install as it is to add a contact" [[2]](#source-2).

BT Servant is not a separately trained model. Its publicly posted engine "integrates OpenAI models plus ChromaDB-backed resources," according to the project's code page [[7]](#source-7). In plain terms, it runs on a general-purpose AI model from OpenAI, but it is told to look up its answers on a curated shelf of Bible translation resources first. Engineers call this retrieval-augmented generation.

Lindsley describes it with a picture: "It's as though when BT Servant is asked a question, he looks back at his desk, and he has a bunch of books on it" [[2]](#source-2). The tool is instructed to "pull the answer from these trusted resources," he says [[2]](#source-2). The site describes its answers as "Grounded in vetted biblical resources" and says the tool "Supports pastors, teachers, and translators. Never replaces them." [[6]](#source-6)

**What is still unproven.** Every claim about BT Servant's reliability comes from unfoldingWord. We found no independent test or published error rate. Lindsley says that for most of its life "it's just been me," with other developers joining in recent months, and "We're constantly adding improvements and fixing things" [[2]](#source-2). Even Mission Network News asks readers to pray that users "will use BT Servant with discernment" [[2]](#source-2).

**The line OpenAI draws.** OpenAI's report, as quoted by the Christian Post, says AI should not become "an arbiter of religious truth or a replacement for spiritual guidance." It adds: "Those roles belong to individuals, religious leaders, and congregations" [[3]](#source-3). Relevant quotes the first part of that line [[4]](#source-4).

## Also Encouraging: A New Center and a Bible App Built on Connections

**Calvin University** is establishing the Calvin Center for Faith and Technology in its School of Engineering and Computer Science, Crosswalk reported on Oct 20 [[8]](#source-8). Led by director Derek Schuurman, the center says it will help Christians engage technology "with courage, curiosity, wisdom, and discernment," and will offer resources, workshops and panels [[9]](#source-9).

**John 1:1**, a free iPhone Bible app from Repertorium LLC, lets readers swipe sideways to follow "a quotation, an echo, a shared person or place" to another passage [[10]](#source-10). It lists 292,374 ranked connections and 10 translations in 7 languages, and promises "No ads. No accounts. No in-app purchases." [[10]](#source-10) We found no independent reviews of it yet.

## Wisdom Corner: Confident Answers Can Still Misquote the Word

A chatbot can sound sure of itself and still get a verse wrong. In a March interview, YouVersion CEO Bobby Gruenewald estimated that the best AI model "misquotes Scripture at least 15% of the time," and some models "as much as 60% of the time" [[11]](#source-11). The article gives no method for that estimate. His larger worry is that readers "might not recognize when a verse is misquoted or subtly altered" [[11]](#source-11). He urges people to know the Bible themselves and to seek guidance from trained pastors [[11]](#source-11).

Even OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, posted on Oct 3: "I am very uncomfortable about people trying to ascribe religious force or a surrender of human judgment to AI models, and think it is a real safety issue" [[12]](#source-12).

Acts 17:11 gives the remedy. Receive teaching with "readiness of mind," and keep "examining the Scriptures daily." Do it together with God's people (Hebrews 10:24-25). A tool can point to a passage. Only the Spirit, working through the Word and the Church, forms a disciple.
