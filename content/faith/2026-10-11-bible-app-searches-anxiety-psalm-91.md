## Reflection: Bring the anxious thing to God

Paul wrote Philippians to a church he loved, and the letter keeps returning to joy. Just before this week's verse, he tells the believers to rejoice in the Lord always and reminds them that the Lord is near (Philippians 4:4-5).

Then he turns to worry. "In nothing be anxious" can sound like an order to stop feeling something. Read the rest of the sentence. Paul does not tell the church to bury its fears. He tells it where to take them: "in everything, by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God."

The promise that follows is not that every problem will vanish. It is that "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your thoughts in Christ Jesus." The peace belongs to God. It keeps watch over the heart, and it is found in Christ.

This week's feature reports that "anxiety" was one of the most-searched words in a popular Bible app this year [[1]](#source-1). That is a tender detail. Many people are bringing their worry to Scripture. Paul would point them one step further, from the page to the Person, from reading about peace to asking God for it.

Here is one practice for this week. When a worry rises, name it to God in a short prayer. Add one thing you are thankful for, and leave the request with him. If the worry is heavy, tell a brother or sister in your church so they can pray with you.

## Feature: Anxiety, hope and healing were among a Bible app's top searches this year

YouVersion says "anxiety," "hope" and "healing" were among the most-searched terms in its Bible App this year, and that "Psalm 91" was the single most-searched term of all [[1]](#source-1). The ministry released the data on Oct 6 [[1]](#source-1).

The release reports that daily use reached 18.5 million people, up more than 13% from 2025 [[1]](#source-1). Every figure in this story is self-reported by YouVersion. No outside group has audited them, and the release does not define "daily use" or explain how search terms were counted.

**What people brought to Scripture.** Psalm 91 is a psalm of trust in God as refuge and shelter, so its place at the top fits the other searches. The pattern is not new. YouVersion's 2025 data listed love, anxiety and peace among the most-searched terms, and named Isaiah 41:10, where God tells his people not to fear, as the most engaged-with verse [[2]](#source-2). Two years running, the company's own numbers show people opening the Bible when they are afraid.

"This year, we've been encouraged by how many people are turning to Scripture for answers to their questions," said Bobby Gruenewald, YouVersion's founder and CEO [[1]](#source-1).

**How the app reaches people.** YouVersion is a digital ministry of Life.Church, and its Bible apps are free [[1]](#source-1). Its Family of Apps offers 3,800 versions of the Bible in 2,400 languages, and it works with more than 45,000 churches and partners [[1]](#source-1). After passing one billion installs last November, it added more than 160 million new installs in 2026, the company says [[1]](#source-1).

**What comes next.** November is Global Bible Month, built around the 30-Day Bible Challenge. Participants are encouraged to read every day, "whether through a favorite print Bible or digitally through an app" [[1]](#source-1). YouVersion says nearly 3 million people signed up for the Challenge's first year in 2025 [[1]](#source-1). This year the Challenge adds a feature that lets users "track their progress and see how their friends are engaging alongside them" [[1]](#source-1).

That sign-up figure should not be set beside last year's report, which said "more than 2.6 million people took" the Challenge [[2]](#source-2). Signing up and taking part are different measures.

**What the numbers can't show.** A search records a need. It does not record what happens next: whether a reader prays, comes back, or finds a church. The unit YouVersion counts is the app being opened. Its 2025 record day had "more than 19 million people opening the app in a single day" [[2]](#source-2).

The data is also personal. YouVersion's privacy policy lists "search history" among the information it collects [[3]](#source-3). The company says it does not sell or share personal data with third-party advertisers, and that it publishes engagement stats only "in an aggregated form" [[3]](#source-3). Those are the company's own commitments, not the findings of an outside audit.

The app's design has drawn questions before. A 2013 essay from the University of Chicago Divinity School's Sightings argued that, with YouVersion, engagement with religious content is being shaped by "the same methods advertisers use to compel us to buy and consume their products" [[4]](#source-4). The new friend-tracking feature is a social one. Readers can weigh whether seeing a friend's progress encourages them to read or simply keeps score.

## Also Encouraging: A dyslexia-friendly Bible and an offline Bible prototype

**A Bible set in a typeface built with dyslexic readers.** Ascension has released the Catholic Reading Bible: Dyslexia-Friendly Edition, which it calls "the first complete Catholic Bible to feature the Grace typeface" [[5]](#source-5). Ascension says Grace was developed by the Bible design firm 2K/DENMARK, "informed by research, testing with dyslexic readers, and work involving the University of Cambridge," and that the design uses lines of no more than 35 characters, ragged-right text and 11-point type [[5]](#source-5). Christianity Today reported in 2024 that Grace was first used in Bibles from Lifeway and Crossway, and that its designer made the font nonproprietary [[6]](#source-6). One caveat: research on earlier dyslexia fonts has found little evidence that they help people read faster or more accurately [[7]](#source-7), and we found no published peer-reviewed results for Grace itself.

**An offline, private Bible from a hackathon.** Gloo's AI Hackathon gave its Best of Bible Track award, powered by YouVersion, to Envoy Mission, which Gloo describes as an offline, private Bible delivered through multilingual AI conversations with seekers [[8]](#source-8). Track winners received $20,000 each, from a field of more than 1,000 builders on more than 115 teams [[8]](#source-8). Envoy Mission is a hackathon prototype, and Gloo's release is the only public description of it we found.

## Wisdom Corner: When AI video turns the Word into content

The quieter risk is treating Scripture as something to consume. On Oct 6, Pray.com launched Pray Production Studio, an AI tool that turns a sermon into cinematic video, with plans starting at $19 a month [[9]](#source-9). In 2025, Jeffrey Bilbro, an English professor at Grove City College, told NPR that the company's AI Bible videos situate the Bible "as entertainment, as content to be titillated or amused by," rather than "a word that is a divine revelation intended to transform our lives" [[10]](#source-10). Brad East of Abilene Christian University said the videos rob the Bible of its power by reducing it to an action movie [[10]](#source-10).

Scripture asks for more than hearing. James warns against listening to the word without doing it (James 1:22). This week's verse points the same way. Paul does not promise peace through more input. He sends the anxious to prayer, and he locates God's peace "in Christ Jesus." A search or a video can be a first step. The next steps are prayer, obedience and life with a local church.

## Prayer: For anxious hearts

Father, when worry rises in us, teach us to bring it to you with thanksgiving, and guard our hearts and thoughts in Christ Jesus. Let every screen we open lead us back to you and to your people. Amen.
