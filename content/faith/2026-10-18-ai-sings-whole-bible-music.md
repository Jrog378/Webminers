## Reflection: Words on the heart, talked over at home

Deuteronomy records Moses' last words to God's people as a new generation prepares to go on without him. Just before this week's verse, he gives the call Jesus later named the greatest commandment: love the LORD your God with all your heart, soul and might (Deuteronomy 6:4-5; Matthew 22:37-38).

Then Moses says how that love takes root. "These words, which I command you today, shall be on your heart." Not only on a scroll or a shelf. On the heart.

The way the words get there is ordinary. God's people are to talk of them "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise up." The whole day becomes a place to remember.

Notice who does the teaching. Parents are told to "teach them diligently to your children." Scripture passes from one person to another in the middle of daily life: at the table, on the road, at bedtime.

This week's feature looks at two projects that use AI to sing every chapter of the Bible. A song can carry God's words into the car or the kitchen, the very places Moses named. That can help a verse stick. But the passage puts the words on the heart and the teaching in the home, and Moses hands the talking to parents, not to a speaker on the counter.

Here is one practice for this week. Pick one short passage. Read it aloud at a meal, say it on a walk and pray it before you sleep. If you have children or grandchildren, ask what they think it means. If music helps you remember it, sing it, and then talk about it together.

## Feature: A month of AI setting the whole Bible to music, word for word

Two projects, The Music Bible and TheSinging.Bible, say AI now sings every chapter of the Bible, but we found no published error rates from either [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). This is a look back at the past month, not this week's news. Between mid-September and mid-October, two separate projects that use AI to sing every chapter of the Bible kept growing, and new survey data showed why many pastors remain cautious.

TheSinging.Bible, which launched on Aug 25, added an iPhone app on Sept 21, a round-the-clock YouTube radio stream on Oct 2 and an Android app on Oct 4 [[3]](#source-3). As of Oct 4, it lists 52,316 recordings: all 1,189 chapters in 22 styles, each sung in a male and a female voice [[3]](#source-3). Answered Faith reported in September that it launched with 38,048 recordings [[1]](#source-1).

We noted The Music Bible's app launch in our Sep 30 issue. Since then, on Oct 8, Crosswalk ran a guest commentary on the app, which is led by founder Micah Fiechter [[4]](#source-4). Crosswalk reported Fiechter saying the whole Bible is available in 11 styles, and it put the app at more than 30,000 songs, with three genres in Spanish [[4]](#source-4). Three days later, the headline of Fiechter's own op-ed put the figure at 38,000 [[5]](#source-5). The app's homepage counter now reads 38,556 [[6]](#source-6). The counts differ by source and date, and we found no outside check of them.

| | The Music Bible | TheSinging.Bible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | 38,556 songs, per its site counter [[6]](#source-6) | 52,316 recordings as of Oct 4 [[3]](#source-3) |
| Styles | 14 complete-Bible genres, counting Spanish [[6]](#source-6) | 22 styles, two voices each [[3]](#source-3) |
| English text | Not named on the pages we read | "Singing Bible Edition," based on the Berean Standard Bible [[3]](#source-3) |
| AI tools | Suno and ChatGPT, among others [[2]](#source-2) | "AI-empowered technologies," tools not named [[3]](#source-3) |
| Cost | Free on iOS and Android [[4]](#source-4) | Free, no sign-up, no ads [[3]](#source-3) |

**How they say they protect the text.** The Music Bible says AI shapes the music, not the words. It pledges it "will never use artificial intelligence to create songs that interpret, paraphrase, or extrapolate the meaning of Scripture" [[2]](#source-2). It says verses stay in order with no choruses, though "occasionally, a word or phrase may be repeated for musical expression" [[2]](#source-2). TheSinging.Bible makes a narrower claim: "every recording follows the written text, which is shown with every chapter" [[3]](#source-3). The project says producers, engineers and quality-control reviewers were responsible for reviewing the recordings [[1]](#source-1).

"We don't change Scripture to fit music; we change music to fit Scripture," Fiechter told Crosswalk [[4]](#source-4). The Music Bible argues that setting the whole Bible, "over 750,000 words," to music was more than any single artist or team could do by hand [[2]](#source-2).

**Who it is for.** Both projects are built for listening, and The Music Bible also pitches memorizing [[6]](#source-6). TheSinging.Bible says under 20 minutes a day sings a listener through the whole Bible in a year, with the text on screen [[7]](#source-7). Its book of Jonah is even available as a sea shanty [[7]](#source-7). The Music Bible app offers read-along lyrics and playlists that AI builds from a short text request [[6]](#source-6). "I don't want people to remember Music Bible; I want them to remember the Bible," Fiechter said [[4]](#source-4).

**What is not yet known.** We found no published error rates from either project. Answered Faith noted that TheSinging.Bible's release did not "publish detailed error rates from its quality-control process" [[1]](#source-1). The Music Bible commits to "at least 100% accuracy in the oral representation of each passage," but its page describes no review method [[2]](#source-2). Reach is self-reported too. TheSinging.Bible's one million plays in two weeks come from its own analytics, and "a play should not be confused with a unique listener or a completed chapter" [[1]](#source-1).

**How pastors see AI in worship music.** On Oct 6, Barna reported that 33% of Protestant pastors feel positive about AI writing worship songs and 29% about AI interpreting scripture, compared with 64% for AI creating small-group curriculum [[8]](#source-8). The survey of 442 pastors was conducted online in December 2025 [[8]](#source-8). We found no mention of sung Scripture in Barna's report. The Music Bible says AI writes only the music, so it falls between these categories.

## Also Encouraging: AI translation with expert review for The Chosen, and a sound board that takes plain language

**The Chosen, with expert review.** On Oct 13, Movieguide followed up on how AI helps translate The Chosen. Citing a fact sheet, it reports that for subtitles "a trained subject matter expert examines each line for accuracy, biblical fidelity and cultural relevance" [[9]](#source-9). The wider workflow was first reported by Deseret News in May: Come and See uses an AI app for a first-pass translation in minority languages, then speech-to-speech AI lays the original actor's voice over recordings made by linguists [[10]](#source-10). The goal is 600 languages, more than 500 of them with no existing dubbing infrastructure [[10]](#source-10).

**Plain-language help at the sound desk.** At Gloo's fourth annual AI hackathon, held Oct 6-8, Biola University's team Holy Sound reached the finals with a tool that lets people use natural language to run sound boards [[11]](#source-11). ChurchLeaders reports it is "specifically designed to help non-technical people use Ableton Live during church services" [[11]](#source-11).

## Wisdom Corner: A melody is never neutral

Even when every word is unchanged, a song shapes how we hear a passage. "Pastors should also make clear that a musical performance is not a new form of biblical authority. Melody involves interpretation," Duke Taber wrote for Answered Faith [[1]](#source-1). He warned that AI "can mispronounce names, place emphasis in strange locations or produce a musical mood that fights against a passage's meaning" [[1]](#source-1). Plays, he added, "cannot measure repentance, understanding, obedience or growing love for Christ" [[1]](#source-1). Kenny Lamm of NC Baptists drew the line in January, telling Baptist Press that AI "can assist the craft but cannot replace the heart and witness of human worshippers" [[12]](#source-12).

Paul tells believers to let the word of Christ dwell in them richly as they teach one another with psalms, hymns and spiritual songs (Colossians 3:16). In that picture, singing Scripture is something the church does together. A recording can carry the words into a commute. It cannot replace reading the Bible, gathered worship or the voices of God's people.
